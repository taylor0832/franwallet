# SBA vs. ROBS vs. conventional: the three ways to fund a franchise

Most first units get funded one of three ways. Each has a distinct trade: the SBA trades paperwork for accessibility, ROBS trades your retirement for debt-free capital, and conventional trades a higher bar for speed and flexibility.

## SBA 7(a): the default for a reason

The SBA doesn't lend money — it guarantees a chunk of a bank's loan to you, which makes banks willing to fund first-time operators they'd otherwise decline.

- **Typical shape**: up to $5M, 10-year terms for a franchise build-out (25 with real estate), rates commonly prime + 2–3%.
- **What it takes**: roughly **10–20% down** from you, a credit score generally 680+, and a personal guarantee — plus a lien on your house if you have equity. Yes, really.
- **Timeline**: 45–90 days from application to funding. Working with an SBA *Preferred* lender who already knows your brand meaningfully shortens it.
- **The franchise angle**: lenders finance recognizable brands with strong Item 20 histories far more readily. Your FDD becomes part of the loan file.

**Best for**: buyers with decent credit and a down payment who want to keep their retirement accounts intact.

## ROBS: your 401(k) buys the business

Rollovers as Business Startups: a structure where your retirement funds roll into a new C-Corp's retirement plan, which then buys stock in the corporation — funding the business with your own 401(k)/IRA money, **without the early-withdrawal penalty or a loan payment**.

- **Real advantages**: no debt service during the ramp (the #1 killer of year-one units), no credit requirement, fast (2–4 weeks).
- **Real costs**: setup runs ~$5k plus ~$100–150/month administration, you must operate as a C-Corp, run payroll for yourself, and stay compliant forever — the IRS looks closely at these.
- **The real risk**: if the business fails, your retirement went with it. Debt is painful; a vaporized 401(k) at 55 is a different category of painful.

**Best for**: buyers with $100k+ in retirement accounts, thin liquidity outside them, and clear-eyed acceptance of the downside. Most people should pair a *partial* ROBS with a smaller SBA loan rather than betting the whole account.

## Conventional / everything else

Straight bank loans (rare for first units without collateral or an existing banking relationship), HELOCs (cheap money, house on the line — which an SBA guarantee often takes anyway), equipment financing layered on top of any structure, and franchisor financing programs (check Item 10 of the FDD — some are genuinely good, some are a rate you'd never accept from a bank).

**Best for**: multi-unit operators with a track record, or buyers with strong collateral who value speed over rate.

## How to actually decide

1. **Count your liquid capital** vs. the top of the FDD Item 7 range *plus six months of operating losses*. If the gap is small, conventional or cash. If the gap is real, SBA. If your net worth lives in a 401(k), ROBS enters the conversation.
2. **Price the monthly payment into the P&L** at the FDD's median revenue — and at 80% of it. A loan the unit can only service at median is a loan you can't afford.
3. **Talk to lenders before you sign the franchise agreement.** A pre-qualification letter also tells you what the lending market thinks of your brand — free due diligence.
